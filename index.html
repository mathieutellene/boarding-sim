Agent-based simulation · passenger boarding

How long it takes to fill an aircraft, and why

Every passenger is an agent with their own walking speed, carry-on bags and seat. The aisle is single file and nobody overtakes — everything else follows from that. Fifteen ways to board the same aircraft, racing side by side on the same passenger manifest.

Why boarding is worth modelling

Boarding is the longest task in a narrow-body turnaround, the one with the most variance, and the only one whose duration is set almost entirely by a queueing rule an airline chooses for free.

105,674 commercial flights a day OAG, 2026 average. Peak day: 153,359 (Flightradar24, 23 Jul 2026)
€166 per minute of delay at the gate EUROCONTROL standard inputs, 2022 prices. €45/min for delays under 30 min
~9pax/min boarding rate today Down from ~20 before 1970. Attributed to Boeing, widely cited
2× longer than in the 1970s ~35 min for 140 passengers today vs ~15 min then. Fuller aircraft, more carry-on

The process got worse while the aircraft stayed the same. An A320 cabin is the same tube it always was, but load factors climbed and checked-bag fees pushed luggage into the overhead bins. Both changes hit the same bottleneck: one person stowing a bag stops everyone behind them, because a single aisle cannot be overtaken.

What the model says. On the default A320 at 92% load, the method airlines actually announce — back-to-front blocks — takes 15.5 min. The best method here takes 10.8 min. That gap, 4.7 minutes per turn, costs nothing to capture: it is the same aircraft, the same passengers and the same gate staff, reading the boarding groups in a different order.

The honest caveat. Those minutes are not worth 4.7 × €166. Boarding normally sits inside the scheduled turnaround, so the saving only converts into money when it stops a turn from overrunning its slot — or when it lets an airline schedule a shorter turn in the first place. The delay-cost figure is the scale of what an overrun costs, not a per-flight prize. Anyone quoting the multiplication is selling something.

Does the model reproduce reality? Run with back-to-front, it boards at 10.7 passengers per minute against the ~9 reported in industry data. Right magnitude, slightly optimistic — which is what you would expect from a model with no gate-agent friction, no last-minute bag checks and nobody walking to the wrong row.

    What is inside the model

    The aisle rules everything

    Each passenger walks at their own speed until they hit the minimum spacing behind the person in front. Nobody overtakes, so the order in the aisle is exactly the order they crossed the door. One stopped passenger halts everyone behind them.

    Two ways to stop the queue

    Stowing: opening the bin and placing a bag blocks the aisle for a few seconds per bag, with substantial variance.

    Seat interference: if you have a window seat and people are already sitting between you and the aisle, they have to get up and step out. The cost grows with each person standing: this is the penalty that window-first methods remove outright.

    Groups

    Passengers travelling together board together and sit together. Among themselves the stand-up manoeuvre is cheaper — they coordinate — but they still occupy the aisle while doing it.

    Why back-to-front disappoints

    It concentrates an entire block into a few adjacent rows: people pile into their own zone while the rest of the aisle sits empty. The methods that win spread passengers along the whole tube so several can stow at the same time.

    Where Steffen's advantage actually comes from

    The alternating-rows model exists to do a subtraction. It spreads people along the tube exactly like Steffen, but avoids not one single seat interference. On the default A320: random takes 13.5 minutes, alternating rows 12.5 and Steffen 10.8. Spreading along the aisle explains a little over a third of the difference; the remaining two thirds is purely nobody having to stand up. That is why window-middle-aisle, which spreads nothing, lands so close to Steffen.

    Fixing half the problem barely helps

    Adding “window first” inside each zone removes almost all of back-to-front's interference and improves it by 11%. It is still worse than sorting nobody, because the zone crowding is untouched. Both faults have to be attacked at once — which is what the reverse pyramid does, and it does reach the top of the table.

    Queue compliance

    The fine-grained orderings assume 180 people line up in an exact sequence. On the default A320, Steffen drops to 7.9 minutes with a perfect queue, but gives up two thirds of its lead over window-middle-aisle by 60% compliance — 11.1 against 11.6 — and loses 51% of its own performance across the range. Window-first moves 7% end to end, because it only ever asks for three lines. That robustness, not its peak, is why airlines use the second and not the first.

    Sometimes disobedience helps

    The same experiment in reverse: back-to-front improves as compliance falls, from 16.3 to 12.8 minutes. Scrambling a bad order moves it towards random, and random spreads people along the whole aisle. A method is only worth having if it survives people not following it.

    What it does not model

    A single aisle only (no 3-4-3 widebody cabins), no carts or crew, no bags that do not fit and get gate-checked, and nobody walking to the wrong row. The default times sit in the range reported in the boarding literature, but they are parameters: move them.
