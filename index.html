Fifteen ways to fill an aircraft

Boarding is a queueing problem with one aisle, no overtaking, and two ways to stop the line: someone stowing a bag, and someone climbing over the people already sitting between them and the aisle. Every boarding method ever announced over a PA is an attempt to schedule those two events so they block as few people as possible. All fifteen run here at once, on the identical passenger manifest.

Why this is worth modelling

Boarding is the longest and most variable task in a narrow-body turnaround, and the only one whose duration is set almost entirely by a queueing rule the airline chooses for free.

Boarding is the bottleneck of the turn

A turnaround runs deplaning, baggage, fuelling, catering, cleaning and boarding against the clock. Operations research keeps finding the same thing: boarding contributes more timing uncertainty than any other component, which makes it the task that decides whether the aircraft leaves on time. Turnarounds that took 10 minutes at Southwest in the 1970s now take 42 to 49.

Two costs, and only one of them is linear

Fuel and CO₂ convert directly. The APU runs throughout boarding. Five minutes saved is five minutes of an auxiliary turbine not burning 150–200 kg/h, every single flight, whether or not the departure time changes. That saving is real and it compounds across a fleet.

Delay cost does not. Boarding normally sits inside the scheduled turn, so minutes saved only become money when they stop a turn overrunning its slot, or when they let an airline schedule a shorter turn. Multiplying minutes saved by €/min overstates the case, badly. This simulator reports both, labelled.

The industry made it worse on purpose

Checked-bag fees arrived in 2008 and pushed luggage into the cabin — $7.3 bn of US ancillary revenue in 2024, and a permanent shortage of overhead bin space. That shortage produced bin anxiety, and bin anxiety produced gate lice: passengers crowding the gate before their group is called, which the Cambridge Dictionary added in 2025. Cabin crew in most US contracts are not allowed to lift passengers' bags, because of the injury rate. So the bottleneck stays.

Which is why the optimum is not used

Ancillary revenue is a $55 bn market. Priority boarding sells passengers an escape from friction the airline itself created, and it underwrites co-branded credit cards worth billions more. Calling a scattered group of high-value passengers first destroys any careful ordering — and airlines accept that, deliberately, because the ancillary revenue outweighs the minutes. The optimum loses to the business model, not to the physics.

It is all changing right now

United reinstated window-middle-aisle in October 2023 and measured up to two minutes per flight, with an override that keeps families on one booking together. Southwest ends 53 years of open seating on 27 January 2026. Boeing's Space Bins add 50% more bin volume — 31,000 gate-checks avoided and 110 block hours released per aircraft per year. Biometric gates clear a passenger in 6–10 seconds, 60% faster than a manual check.

What this model is for

It does not tell an airline what to do. It isolates the part of the problem that is pure physics — how an ordering rule interacts with one aisle and a set of bins — so the cost of the commercial decisions layered on top can be read off in minutes, kilograms and dollars instead of argued about.

    What is inside the model

    The aisle rules everything

    Each passenger walks at their own speed until they hit the minimum spacing behind the person in front. Nobody overtakes, so the order in the aisle is exactly the order they crossed the door, and one stopped passenger halts everyone behind them.

    Two ways to stop the queue

    Stowing blocks the aisle for a few seconds per bag, with substantial variance. Seat interference happens when people are already sitting between you and the aisle: they have to get up and step out, and the cost grows with each one. That is the penalty window-first methods remove outright.

    Where Steffen's advantage comes from

    The alternating-rows method exists to do a subtraction. It spreads people along the tube exactly like Steffen but avoids not one single interference, so the gap between the two is the price of interference alone. Spreading explains about a third of Steffen's lead; the other two thirds is nobody standing up. That is why window-middle-aisle, which spreads nothing, lands within 5% of the optimum.

    Compliance decides the ranking

    The fine-grained orderings assume people line up in an exact sequence. Steffen does 7.9 minutes with a perfect queue and 11.9 when they do not — it loses 51% of its own performance. Window-first moves 7% across the same range. And back-to-front improves as people stop following it, because scrambling a bad order moves it towards random. A method is only worth having if it survives people not following it.

    What it does not model

    A single aisle only, so no widebodies. No crew, carts, wheelchairs or gate-checked bags — bags always fit. Nobody walks to the wrong row or forgets anything. Default times sit in the range reported in the boarding literature, but they are parameters: move them.

    How honest the numbers are

    Run with back-to-front, the model boards at about 10.7 passengers per minute against the ~9 reported in industry data — the right magnitude, roughly 19% optimistic. Treat differences between methods as meaningful and absolute times as a lower bound.

    Discrete-time agent-based model: 50 ms steps on screen, 100 ms in the replay.
